01 · The principle

Coordination is what makes trades work together.

A construction project isn't built by one team. It's built by specialists — electricians, plumbers, HVAC engineers, masons, finishers, joiners — each of whom is expert in their own area but has to fit into a wider sequence.

Subcontractor coordination is what makes that sequence work. Clear scope per trade, a defined schedule, defined responsibilities, and a single coordinator holding the connections — that's what keeps quality from slipping between trades.

What coordination covers

Six areas that keep trades working together.

Subcontractor coordination isn't one task — it's a set of responsibilities that run across every trade and every stage.

01 / 06

Scope definition per trade

Each subcontractor works to a defined scope — what they own, what they don't, and how their work connects to the trades on either side. Scope clarity is what stops quality gaps at the boundaries between trades.

02 / 06

Quality standards

A consistent expectation of workmanship across trades — not different standards on different parts of the same project.

03 / 06

Safety alignment

Site safety expectations applied consistently — across trades, not trade-by-trade.

04 / 06

Schedule visibility

Each trade knows when they're on site, what stage precedes them and what follows — not left to guess.

05 / 06

Interface coordination

The points where trades meet — walls and services, framing and openings, finishes and fittings — actively managed.

06 / 06

Documentation & handover

Every trade's work recorded as it's completed — reviewed against the agreed scope, checked before the next trade moves in, and documented for the final handover. Records that hold up if a question arises later.

The workflow

How a subcontractor engagement moves forward.

From the first conversation about scope to the final review of completed work, each stage has defined responsibilities on both sides.

Stage 01

Scope agreement

What the trade is responsible for, what they aren't, and how their work connects to others — agreed in writing.

Stage 02

Schedule & sequence

When the trade is on site, what precedes them and what follows — communicated in advance, not on the day.

Stage 03

On-site coordination

Interface points between trades actively managed. Issues raised early, not after the fact.

Stage 04

Quality review

Work checked against the agreed scope and standard before the next trade moves into the same area.

Stage 05

Documentation & close-out

Completed work recorded, any outstanding items resolved, and the trade's involvement formally closed.

Why it matters

What good coordination prevents.

On a project without clear subcontractor coordination, problems rarely come from one trade being bad. They come from the boundaries between trades — where ownership is unclear, sequence is rushed, and issues aren't caught in time.

What unclear coordination causes
Ownership gaps

Things that "aren't my trade" fall between the cracks — and get discovered at handover.

Rework at interfaces

One trade covers another's work, and it has to be opened up again to fix.

Site collisions

Trades arriving at the same time to the same area, tripping over each other.

Unclear accountability

When something goes wrong, no one is clearly responsible — so it stays wrong.

What coordinated delivery delivers
Defined ownership

Every part of the work is owned by a named party — with boundaries agreed in advance.

Clean interfaces

Where trades meet, work is coordinated so nothing has to be re-opened later.

Sequenced trades

Trades move through the site in order, with clear slots so no one is tripping over anyone else.

Clear accountability

When something needs to move, it's clear whose responsibility it is to move it.
